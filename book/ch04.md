# Chapter 4: What Sells in Two Weeks, Not Five Months

You can do everything else in this book right and still fail if you buy the wrong things. The wrong things are not bad clothes. They are clothes nobody is searching for this week.

This chapter is a short list of what moves fast, why it moves, and the one habit that keeps you from guessing.

## The habit first: sold comps, every time

Before you buy anything, even a two-dollar shirt, open the eBay app, search the brand and item, and tap the filter for "Sold Items." Not active listings. Sold. Active listings are what sellers hope to get. Sold listings are what buyers actually paid.

Look at the last ten sales. Three questions:

1. **How many sold in the last 30 days?** Five or more means there is a market. One or two means you will be waiting.
2. **What did the cheapest third sell for?** That is your real price, not the top one.
3. **How does my item compare?** Same size range, same condition, same era. A medium sells faster than an extra small. No holes sells faster than "small hole, see photo."

This takes forty seconds in the aisle. It is the difference between a shirt that sells in nine days and a shirt that becomes a bin ornament. Do it every single time. The sellers who skip this step are the ones you see on forums asking why nothing sells.

## The fast five

These categories sell inside two weeks when priced right. They are not the most profitable things you could ever find. They are the most reliable, and reliable is what you need when you are starting from nothing.

### 1. Vintage sports tees and sweatshirts

Pro and college teams from the 80s and 90s. The makers to look for are printed on the tag: Starter, Nutmeg Mills, Salem Sportswear, Logo 7, Logo Athletic, Chalk Line, Apex One. A Nutmeg tee from 1992 with a single-stitch hem is a twenty-five to forty dollar item that sells in a week to a buyer who searched the team name. Thick boxy cotton means 90s. Thin fifty-fifty blend means 80s and often sells higher. We cover how to spot these fast in Chapter 7.

Why they move: buyers search by team, and every team has fans with money. A Bulls, Cowboys, or Saints search happens thousands of times a day. Your shirt is one of only a handful in that size.

### 2. Levi's 501 and 505 jeans

Every thrift store has a rack of them. Look for the red tab, a leather or paper patch on the back waistband, and "Made in USA" if you can find it. Measure the actual waist and inseam, because the tag lies on vintage denim. Price 501s at twenty-five to forty-five dollars, more for USA-made with a good fade.

Why they move: men buy jeans by waist and inseam, and they want the pair that fits. A thirty-four by thirty-two in good shape sells in days to someone who searched exactly that.

### 3. Carhartt and Dickies work jackets

The chore coat, the detroit jacket, the duck vest. Carhartt is the single most searched workwear brand on every resale platform. Look for the older tags with "Made in USA" and the square-ish logo. Faded and worn is fine. Thrashed with a torn pocket still sells, just lower. Price at forty to a hundred dollars depending on style and condition.

Why they move: they are fashion now, not just workwear. Buyers in cities pay for the fade you find in a small-town thrift store for six dollars.

### 4. Nike, Adidas, and Starter jackets

Windbreakers, satin bombers, pullovers with the big logo. The 90s Nike swoosh windbreaker in a bold color sells at thirty to sixty dollars in days. Starter satin team jackets are the top of this category and can run a hundred and fifty or more. Check the zipper works and the lining is not shredded.

Why they move: the look is everywhere on Depop. Teenage and twenty-something buyers search these brands by name every day.

### 5. Brand-name sneakers in good shape

Nike, New Balance, Hoka, Asics, Birkenstock. Not rare pairs, just clean ones. A thrifted pair of New Balance 990s or Hoka Cliftons at eight dollars resells at forty to eighty. Clean them properly, photograph the soles and the inside tag, state the size exactly.

Why they move: shoes are a size search. Someone who wears a ten and a half and wants Hokas will find yours tonight.

## What does not move, and why

**Mall brands from the last ten years.** Old Navy, Gap, American Eagle. Millions of them exist and every one is three dollars on Poshmark. Skip them unless it is a true vintage piece from the 90s.

**Formal wear.** Suits, dress shirts, bridesmaid dresses. Nobody buys these on a whim and sizing is unforgiving. Slow and heavy to ship.

**Anything you can't identify.** No tag, no brand, no era. There is no search term that leads a buyer to it. If you cannot name it, you cannot sell it.

**Damaged items you plan to "fix."** You won't. Buy it clean or buy it as a bundle item priced for flaws.

**Kids' clothes.** Low price, high volume, lots of competition. Fine later, wrong now.

## The two-week test

Here is how to know you bought right. After your first ten listings, count how many sold in fourteen days. If it's five or more, your sourcing is good and your pricing is good. If it's two or fewer, one of three things is wrong: you bought outside the fast five, you priced at the top instead of the bottom quarter, or you only listed on one platform. Fix that one thing. Don't buy more inventory until it's fixed.

## A note on what I actually started with

My first order was not a thrift haul. I did not have a bin store within two hours of me. I ordered a ten-piece lot of A-grade vintage graphic tees from a wholesaler for eighty-five dollars, delivered. That is about eight-fifty a shirt. I ran comps on the brands and eras they said were in the lot before I ordered, and I knew that if the average shirt cleared twenty-five dollars I would double my money inside the 45-day rule. Chapter 6 walks through exactly what arrived and what each piece did. The point here is just that I did not guess. I checked.
