# Chapter 1: The 45-Day Money Rule

I lost forty dollars on a drone battery, and it was the best thing that ever happened to my resale business.

Here is what happened. I had a spare battery for a drone I no longer flew. I listed it on eBay at a fair price, and because eBay suggested it, I turned on Promoted Listings at the recommended rate. Then I waited. And waited. Five months later it sold. By the time eBay took its final value fee, then took its promoted listing fee on top of that, then I paid to ship it, I had lost about forty dollars against what I thought I was going to make. Five months of a listing sitting there, and I paid for the privilege.

That sale taught me the only rule in this book that really matters. If you remember nothing else, remember this:

**Money that moves beats money that sits.**

## Why a $28 sale beats a $40 sale

Let me show you with real numbers, because this is where most beginners get it backwards.

Say you buy a vintage graphic tee for six dollars. You check the sold listings on eBay and see it has gone for anywhere from twenty-five to forty-five dollars. The beginner's instinct is to list it at forty dollars and wait for the right buyer. Sometimes that works. Usually that shirt sits for two, three, five months. Eventually it sells for forty and you made thirty-four dollars.

Now take the same shirt and price it at twenty-eight dollars, near the bottom of what it actually sells for. It sells in a week. You made twenty-two dollars. Less, right?

Here is the part that matters. That twenty-two dollars is in your hand in week one, not month five. You put it straight back into three more six-dollar shirts. By week three, those three shirts have sold at twenty-eight dollars each. That's sixty-six dollars of profit from the same starting six dollars, in less time than the "patient" seller waited for a single forty-dollar sale. Do it again and the gap keeps widening. The patient seller made thirty-four dollars once. You made sixty-six, then reinvested that, and you are already past a hundred by the time their one shirt finally sells.

This is the whole game when you are starting with no money. You don't have the cash to let inventory sit. Every dollar tied up in a shirt nobody is buying is a dollar that can't buy the next shirt. Turnover is your only advantage over the big sellers with thousands of listings and warehouses full of stock. They can wait. You can't, and you shouldn't want to.

## The rule in one sentence

Here is the standard I use for every single item:

**Every dollar I spend on inventory should come back to me, doubled, within 45 days.**

Not tripled. Not the maximum possible price. Doubled, in 45 days, then spent again. A six-dollar shirt needs to put twelve dollars or more in my pocket after fees and shipping within a month and a half. If an item can't do that, I either price it lower or I don't buy it.

Forty-five days is not a random number. It is long enough to list something on three platforms, send offers, drop the price once, and still have time to move it through a clearance channel if it stalls. It is short enough that your cash turns over roughly eight times a year. A hundred dollars doubled eight times is not a hundred dollars anymore. It's a business.

## The three traps that kill turnover

Three things stop beginners from moving money. I fell into all of them.

**Trap one: pricing at the top.** When you look at sold listings, your eye goes straight to the highest number. That high sale usually had something yours doesn't: a rare size, a better photo, a buyer who really wanted it that day. Price at the bottom quarter of what actually sold. Chapter 9 covers exactly how to find that number. For now, just know that the right price is lower than you want it to be.

**Trap two: paying for ads.** eBay's Promoted Listings will suggest a rate of eight to twelve percent. That stacks on top of the final value fee, which is already around thirteen and a half percent on clothing. You are now giving away a quarter of every sale before shipping. Nobody finds a thirty-dollar vintage tee because of an ad. They find it by searching the brand and the size. For anything under fifty dollars, never promote. If you ever do, on a seventy-five-dollar-plus piece, set the rate yourself at two percent and not a penny more. My drone battery would have sold just the same without the ad. I paid for nothing.

**Trap three: listing on one platform.** A shirt listed only on eBay has one pool of buyers. The same shirt on eBay, Depop, and Vinted the same afternoon has three. The listing takes eight minutes the first time and two minutes to copy. There is no reason to sell on one platform except habit. We'll set up all three in Chapter 3.

## What "fast" actually looks like

So you know what you're aiming for, here is how quickly each channel moves vintage clothing when it's priced right:

| Channel | How fast it sells | What to put there |
|---|---|---|
| Whatnot live show | Seconds per item | Anything over 30 days old |
| Depop, priced 20% under eBay | Days | Tees, crewnecks, jackets |
| Vinted, no seller fee | Days to two weeks | Anything under $40 |
| eBay 7-day auction ending Sunday night | One week | Items with five or more sold comps |
| Bundles of three to five | Days | Slow singles grouped as a "90s lot" |
| eBay fixed price | Weeks to months | Only $75+ collector pieces |

Look at the bottom row. eBay fixed price, the thing almost every beginner uses for everything, is the slowest channel on the list. It has its place, but that place is a hundred-dollar Starter jacket, not a twenty-dollar tee.

## The free tools that do the work for you

Speed doesn't mean sitting at your phone all day. Three free settings do most of it:

1. **Send offers to watchers.** On eBay, every time someone watches your item, you can send them a private discount. It's free. Send ten to fifteen percent off, every time eBay lets you. This closes more sales than any ad ever will.
2. **Auto-accept offers.** On every platform that allows it, set offers to auto-accept at 85 percent of your asking price. Buyers negotiate with themselves and close the sale while you sleep.
3. **The relist.** On Depop, a relisted item shows up as new in the feed. Every two weeks, relist anything that hasn't sold. It costs nothing.

## Your promise to yourself

Before you buy a single item, I want you to write this down somewhere you'll see it. On the wall above your photo spot is a good place.

*Nothing I own sits for more than 60 days.*

Day 0, it's listed on three platforms. Day 14, offers go out and it gets relisted. Day 30, the price drops fifteen percent. Day 45, it goes in the clearance show. Day 60, it gets bundled with other slow items or donated and written off. Then the bin is empty and ready for something that will actually sell. We'll put this whole schedule on a calendar in Chapter 14. For now, just accept the principle. Stale inventory is not an asset. It is a cost, and you are not rich enough to carry it.

I learned that from a drone battery. You can learn it from this page and skip the five months.
